Research area

Plasticizers & EDCs

Bisphenol A, BPA-free alternatives, and their estrogenic effects on human breast cancer cells — research that influenced US and EU chemical policy.

Background

The problem with plastics and hormones

Chemical exposure that interferes with the body's hormone systems has been linked to breast cancer. Large numbers of pollutants can have oestrogen hormone-like activities and can stimulate breast cancer cell growth. Bisphenols are used in the manufacture of plastic bottles, resins, and the plastic lining of cans.

Bisphenol A (BPA) is now known to mimic the effects of oestrogen. Due to public concerns, industry has been replacing BPA with other bisphenol compounds to produce so-called "BPA-free" products — but the safety of these alternatives had not been rigorously evaluated.

Key findings

BPA-free does not mean safe

We investigated estrogenic effects of bisphenol A (BPA) variants in 3 human breast cancer cell lines (Mesnage et al., 2017b). We found that BPA alternatives are not necessarily less estrogenic than BPA in human breast cancer cells. BPAF, BPB, and BPZ were more estrogenic than BPA.

This study was published in the official journal of the Society of Toxicology, Toxicological Sciences, and highlighted by the Editor-in-Chief on the journal cover. The EPA issued a press release to "highlight the need for testing of replacement chemicals prior to their introduction into commerce to demonstrate that they are safer than the chemical being replaced." Our study subsequently contributed to a new bill by the State of New York.

Current research

£100,000 Breast Cancer UK grant

More recently, Breast Cancer UK awarded a grant of £100,000 to Dr Michael Antoniou and colleagues Dr Robin Mesnage and Dr Ana Rodriguez-Mateos at King's College London, to fund a PhD studentship undertaking biomonitoring of UK residents.

The project identifies levels of bisphenols in blood and urine, and evaluates the potential breast cancer initiating and growth promoting activity of bisphenol mixtures at concentrations relevant to human exposure — providing the first real-world mixture data to inform safe exposure limits.
